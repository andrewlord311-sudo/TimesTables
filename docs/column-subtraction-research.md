# Column subtraction: how KS2 teaches it, and what Column Maths could add

Research for Clara's Column Maths game (`app/columns.js`), 29 Oct 2026. Main
source: the NCETM's Year 3 teacher guide for this exact topic, *Mastery
Professional Development 1.21 Algorithms: column subtraction*. This is what
maths hubs train teachers on, and what White Rose and Power Maths follow.
School calculation policies were used to confirm common practice.

## What the curriculum asks

- **Year 3:** subtract numbers with up to three digits using the formal
  column method.
- **Year 4:** up to four digits.
- **Year 5 and 6:** bigger numbers and decimals, same method.

The method is "decomposition", and schools now call the step **exchanging**,
never "borrowing".

## The teaching sequence (NCETM 1.21)

It is two teaching points, each built up in small steps from concrete to
pictorial to abstract.

### Before starting

- **Subtraction facts and bridging ten** are secure, so each column is a
  known fact, not a count.
- **Mental strategies** come first, for example 65 − 23 = 65 − 20 − 3.
- **Flexible partitioning:** 23 is 2 tens and 3 ones, but also 1 ten and
  13 ones. This is the whole idea behind exchanging, and it is taught on its
  own first. For hundreds it becomes 223 = 200 + 20 + 3 = 100 + 120 + 3 =
  100 + 110 + 13.

### Language (deliberate, and used every time)

- **"Minuend minus subtrahend is equal to the difference."** Children name
  all three parts. The minuend is always on top, and the answer sits inside
  the "large equals" of the two lines.
- **Say "subtract" or "minus", not "take away".** Subtraction covers three
  kinds of problem:
  - **Taking away:** "Ali has £146 and spends £34."
  - **Part of a whole:** "76 books, 32 are fiction, how many are not?"
  - **Difference:** "How much further did Joshua jump?"

  "Take away" fits only the first.
- **Column-by-column sentences:** "The ones column represents five ones
  minus three ones, which is equal to two ones." The tens column is said the
  same way, as six tens minus two tens. Children say these aloud with the
  blocks and then with the digits.

### Teaching point 1: no exchange

1. **Show the same sum several ways side by side:** a part-whole "cherry"
   diagram, a bar model, Dienes blocks and the column layout. Ask "What's the
   same? What's different?"
2. **With Dienes, build only the top number** and remove the bottom number
   from it. This is unlike addition, where both numbers are built.
3. **Keep the removed blocks visible.** They show the check straight away:
   65 − 23 = 42, so 23 + 42 = 65.
4. **Drop the blocks quickly.** They explain the method. They are not a way
   to work out answers.
5. **Use place-value headings** (H T O) at first, then remove them.
6. **Vary the shapes of the sums:**
   - 3-digit minus 2-digit: "nothing is subtracted in the hundreds".
   - Zeros in the numbers: 140 − 120, 146 − 103.
7. **Missing-digit puzzles:** for example, 3 _ 5 − _ 3 _ = 234. Ask "What
   could it be? What couldn't it be? How do you know?"

### Teaching point 2: exchange

1. **Introduce exchange with a game.** Each child starts with 99 in Dienes,
   rolls a die and subtracts. When the ones run out, they swap a ten rod for
   ten ones and slide them across. The last child with blocks left wins.
2. **Then write it down alongside.** For 94 − 6: cross out the 9 and write
   8, write a small 1 beside the 4 to make 14, then 14 − 6 = 8. Children
   must link "one fewer ten" with "the extra 1 in the ones".
3. **Hundreds for tens:** 223 − 142. Ask what is the same and what is
   different from swapping a ten.
4. **Exchanging through a zero is still Year 3:** 404 − 257. There are no
   tens, so a hundred becomes 10 tens, then one of those becomes 10 ones.
   You write 3, then 10 crossed to 9, then 14.
5. **Decide before calculating:** "Which of these need an exchange? Once?
   Twice? Through a zero?" Children judge from the digits alone.
6. **Word problems of all three kinds**, with a part-whole drawing first,
   an estimate, and a check by adding.
7. **When not to use columns:** 776 − 200 is a mental sum. Sort calculations
   into "column" and "in my head".

### The expanded method (used by some schools, not by NCETM)

Many school policies add a halfway layout between mental and compact:

    74 − 27  =  (70 + 4) − (20 + 7)
             =  (60 + 14) − (20 + 7)
             =   40 + 7  =  47

It makes the exchange visible in numbers. It isn't in the National
Curriculum and the NCETM sequence goes straight from Dienes to the compact
layout. Whether to include it depends on what Clara's school does.

## Common mistakes the method is designed to prevent

- **Smaller from larger regardless of position.** For 62 − 48, writing 6 in
  the ones because 8 − 2 = 6. This is the big one.
- **Exchanging but not reducing** the column that gave the ten.
- **Starting on the left.**
- **Misaligned digits** when the numbers have different lengths.
- **Getting stuck on zeros**, especially exchanging through one.
- **Reversing the two numbers.**

## How Column Maths compares today

Already there: the compact layout with H T O headings, exchange boxes above
the top digits, the small "1" beside a digit, crossing out, working
ones-first, and hints that name the column without giving the digit. The
stages are 0, 1 and 2 exchanges, then thousands.

Gaps against the school sequence:

1. **No picture of what an exchange is.** There are no blocks: Clara sees
   digits only, so the "1 ten becomes 10 ones" idea is never shown.
2. **No "do I need to exchange?" decision** as a skill of its own.
3. **Exchange through a zero is deliberately blocked**
   (`colAnalyse`: "no chains through zeros at Year 3"). NCETM teaches it in
   Year 3 (404 − 257).
4. **"Take away" in the stage names and hints.** Schools say "subtract" or
   "minus".
5. **No check by adding,** even though column addition sits next door in
   the same game.
6. **No 2-digit warm-up.** It starts at 3 digits.
7. **Headings never fade.**
8. **No missing-digit puzzles, word problems or "column or mental?" sorting.**

## Ideas for the game, roughly in teaching order

1. **"Build it" stage (no exchange).** Dienes-style blocks drawn above the
   sum, top number only. Clara taps blocks away to match the bottom number,
   and the answer digit fills from what is left. She can say the column
   sentence as she goes, and the taken-away blocks stay faded at the side.
2. **"Swap a ten".** When a column is short, she taps a ten rod. It breaks
   into ten ones that slide across, and the layout writes the crossed-out
   digit and the small 1 at the same moment. Later stages hide the blocks and
   she writes the exchange herself, as now.
3. **Dienes dice mini-game.** Start on 99, roll and subtract, against Pip.
   The last one with blocks wins. This is the NCETM's own way in.
4. **"Exchange spotter".** Show three sums and tap the ones that need an
   exchange (once, twice, through a zero) without working them out.
5. **"Through a zero" stage**, for example 404 − 257, with the blocks first.
6. **"Check it" step.** After a subtraction, flip into column addition to
   add the answer back on. It reuses the existing addition code.
7. **Missing-digit puzzles and short word problems**, including "how much
   more", with a bar model.
8. **Smaller fixes:** say "subtract" or "minus" in place of "take away",
   start with 2-digit sums, and fade the H T O headings once a stage is
   cleared.

One question for Clara's teacher (or the school website's calculation policy):
does the school use the **expanded** layout before the compact one, and do they
draw the exchange as crossing out with a small 1, as above? The game should
look exactly like her exercise book.
